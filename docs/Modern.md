# Modern

*This is Appendix A of the [Players Handbook](Players Handbook.md). The core rules — tests, combat, character creation — are the same for every setting; only the aptitudes, keywords, and equipment change.*

See **[Mixing Settings](Players Handbook.md#7-mixing-settings)** for running more than one at the same table.

---

*Sirens, streetlights, and something in the storm drain. Roughly the present day — near enough that everyone at the table already knows how the world works.*

**Currency:** prices use the same numbers as Section 6. A copper piece is a **dollar**; 1 sp is ten, 1 gp is a hundred. Call it whatever your setting calls it.

**What changes:** the aptitude list below replaces Step 1's, the keywords replace Step 2's suggestions, and the equipment replaces Section 6's. Sections 2 through 5 are unchanged.

## A1. Aptitudes

Choose **2** at character creation, as normal.

### Skill Aptitudes

*Occupations — what you did before this, or still do by day.*

- **Detective**: Advantage on tests to examine a scene, follow a paper trail, or read a suspect. Once per long rest, declare an *intuition* — the GM gives you one true fact about a current mystery
- **Medic**: Stabilise a dying character with no test. Once per short rest, ten minutes of care restores 1d6 + WIS HP
- **Mechanic**: Advantage on tests to repair, modify, or sabotage vehicles, electronics, and machinery. Identify the make and condition of unfamiliar equipment on an INT test DC 12
- **Soldier**: No disadvantage with firearms. Advantage on tests to hold composure under fire or read a tactical situation
- **Researcher**: Advantage on INT tests to recall academic knowledge in your field. Once per long rest, declare *"I studied this"* — the GM provides relevant background on a current topic
- **Journalist**: Advantage on CHA tests to get information out of someone in an interview. Once per adventure, name a contact you know in your beat, and they exist
- **Engineer**: Build or jury-rig a working device from what's on hand. INT test DC 12 and ten minutes for something simple — an alarm, a signal, a shaped charge, a weapon

*Talents — competencies layered onto any character.*

- **Driving**: Advantage on DEX tests to handle a car, bike, or boat at speed
- **Firearms**: No disadvantage with handguns, rifles, and shotguns. Quick Draw works with them
- **Computers**: Advantage on INT tests to use, navigate, or break into computer systems. Recognise malware and basic intrusions on sight
- **Streetwise**: Advantage to find black-market goods, locate criminal contacts, or read a neighbourhood's affiliations
- **Athletics**: Advantage on STR/DEX tests for climbing, running, and moving through built environments at speed
- **Break and Enter**: Advantage to defeat locks and alarms with tools. High-security systems roll normally. Without tools, at disadvantage
- **Move Silently and Unseen**: Advantage to sneak, hide, and remain unnoticed
- **Silver Tongue**: Advantage on CHA to negotiate, de-escalate, or convince — not against someone's core interests
- **Languages**: Two additional languages. Can be taken more than once

### Inherent Aptitudes

*These work exactly as in Section 4 — most modern characters take two Skill Aptitudes and are ordinary people, which is the point. Take an Inherent Aptitude only if your game has something in it that isn't.*

Use the Inherent Aptitudes from Section 4 unchanged. **Natural Weapons**, **Natural Armor**, **Darkvision**, and **Tough** describe a werewolf, a revenant, or whatever your game's strangeness turns out to be, as well as they describe a beastfolk.

## A5. Optional Rules

**Lethal firearms.** The list above keeps guns level with medieval weapons, which suits pulp and horror. For a game where a gunshot is a catastrophe, shift **every** firearm up one die and give non-ballistic armour no AC against them. Apply it to the enemies too — that's the whole point, and it makes a single hallway genuinely frightening.

**Response time.** Modern settings have police, ambulances, and fire crews. When something loud happens, roll a d6: that's how many rounds until sirens are audible. This one die does more to shape modern play than any equipment rule.

**Paper trails.** Everything modern characters do leaves a record — cameras, card transactions, phone location. Rather than tracking it, give the GM one **Trace** token each time the party is careless. Spent later, a Trace means someone found them.

## A6. Tone Notes

### The information problem

This is the appendix's central difficulty and it needs solving at your table before session one.

Fantasy runs on a small information horizon: past a day's walk, everything is rumour, so the GM invents it and no one can check. Modern characters have a phone. Any mystery you improvise can be dissolved by a player who says *"I look it up."*

Don't ban the phone. Answer it. Pick whichever fits:

- **No signal.** Basements, tunnels, storms, rural dead zones, deliberate jamming. Cheap, effective, and a reason to keep moving.
- **The record is wrong.** The information exists and it's false — the file was altered, the obituary was printed for someone still alive. Now the search *is* the clue.
- **Access, not knowledge.** They know the file exists. Getting it means a warrant, a favour, or a break-in. The obstacle moves from knowing to reaching.
- **It isn't written down.** The thing they're chasing predates records, or is deliberately absent from them. A search that returns nothing is itself information.

The last one is the best of the four, and it's why modern horror works: the terrifying result is the empty one.

### Everything else

Modern Rath is not action-hero fiction by default. Characters have the same 10 + CON hit points as everyone else, which is two or three shots. The genre this system naturally produces is **crime, investigation, and horror** — people out of their depth in a world that mostly makes sense. Lean into that before you try to make it *Die Hard*.

Ordinary characters are the strength here. A nurse, a mechanic, and a reporter make a better party than three ex-special-forces operators, because the interesting question is what they're willing to do, not what they're able to.

And the world is documented, populated, and legible in a way a fantasy world isn't — which cuts both ways. Your players know how a hospital works, so you don't have to explain it. They also know what *should* happen when someone calls 911, which means the moment nobody comes is worth ten paragraphs of description.
